\chapter{PART TWO: HURRICANE HELENE – TESTING THE IMPROVED PARAMETRIZATION}
\label{ch:hurricanes}

This chapter examines the results of the optimal configuration of the cold pool parameterization parameters, the impact of the parameterization itself, and an increase in horizontal resolution during another North Atlantic Basin hurricane season in 2024: Helene. The storm occurred between September 24 and September 27. As in previous analyses, we begin with a description of the event, followed by results categorized into three main features: trajectory, intensity, and rainfall, under the workflow outlined in section \ref{sec:section45}. By the end of the section, we will discuss the findings in detail.

\section{Event description - Hurricane Helene}
\label{section61}

Hurricane Helene was a destructive and rapidly intensifying tropical cyclone that hugely impacted the southeastern United States in late September 2024. Originating in the Central American Gyre (western Caribbean), Helene was first classified as a tropical storm at 0600 UTC on 25 September, approximately 50 nautical miles east of Cozumel, Mexico. Figures \ref{fig:besthelene} show the best track trajectory made by the hurricane.

	\vspace{0.5em}

	\label{fig:besthelene} % Label para referenciar no texto
\end{figure}

By 1200 UTC the same day (25 September), Helene steadily strengthened in conducive environmental conditions of low wind shear, high moisture, and very warm sea surface temperatures, intensifying into a hurricane just east of Cancún by 1800 UTC. It later entered the Gulf of Mexico as a Category 1 system on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale.

Helene’s intensification was notably rapid, surpassing the threshold for rapid intensification (> 35 mph increase in wind speed in 24 hours; \cite{nhc_helene2024}). Just six hours later, it reached its peak intensity as a category 4 hurricane, with sustained winds of 120 kt (140 mph), approximately 80 nautical miles south-southwest of the Florida Big Bend region.


The first landfall occurred near Perry, Florida, at approximately 0310 UTC on 27 September. At the time, Helene maintained its Category 4 status and was moving rapidly north-northeastward. The storm brought catastrophic storm surge to coastal communities, with inundation levels reaching 12 to 16 feet (3.7 - 4.9 meters) above ground level in the Big Bend region, particularly devastating Keaton Beach and Steinhatchee. Additional surge heights of 6 to 8 feet (1.8 - 2.4 meters) were observed as far south as Tampa \cite{fsu_helene2024}.

As Helene moved inland on the morning of 27 September, it gradually weakened due to land interaction. By 0900 UTC, the system had been downgraded to a tropical storm while located near Macon, Georgia. Despite the weakening, Helene’s impacts intensified as the storm interacted with a stationary front boundary stretching from the central Gulf Coast to the southern Appalachians. Enhanced low-level convergence and orographic lift contributed to excessive rainfall, particularly in these mountainous regions. Figure \ref{fig:synoptic1} pictures this large-scale interaction.

By 1800 UTC on 27 September, Helene had become post-tropical as it merged with a mid-latitude cutoff\footnote{For a precise definition of this term, please refer to the official glossary provided at: \url{https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=cutoff\%20LOW
}} low over the Tennessee Valley and transitioned into an extratropical cyclone over southern Kentucky. The remnant low persisted until 28 September, performing a slow cyclonic loop before dissipating over north-central Tennessee.

Hurricane Helene resulted in widespread devastation across Florida, Georgia, the Carolinas, and Tennessee. Approximately 4 million people lost power due to infrastructure damage. With at least 250 reported fatalities, 176 of which were direct, Helene became the deadliest hurricane in the contiguous United States since Hurricane Katrina in 2005.

For our simulations, the model was initialized on two different dates to perform a simple sensitivity test regarding the initial conditions: (i) on September 24 at 12 UTC, and (ii) on September 25 at 00 UTC (just twelve hours before the system was classified as a Category 1 hurricane). In the run starting on September 24, the first 12 hours of integration were discarded due to the model's spin-up time.

For the analysis of the storm's trajectory and intensity, we selected the time window up to September 28 at 00 UTC, which corresponds to a point shortly after the system weakened and was reclassified as a tropical storm. For the precipitation analysis, however, we extended the time window to include the entire day of September 28, as it was relevant to the extreme rainfall observed in the Carolinas region. Following Figure \ref{fig:synoptic}, the study period of our simulations is represented by the circle bullet points in the trajectory computed by the best track dataset.

Broadly speaking, the forecasts exhibit a positive CTE behavior during the first 36 hours, meaning they remain east of the best track trajectory. Over time, at least up to landfall, there is a tendency for this CTE to decrease, indicating that the forecasted tracks gradually shift from east to west relative to the reference.

Interestingly, it is in the optimal configuration that a change in trajectory direction is observed after landfall, with CTE values increasing again (i.e., the forecasted tracks shift back toward the east of the reference). It is also noteworthy that, in terms of phase alignment (i.e., whether the forecast is ahead or behind), the inclusion of cold pool parametrization improves this aspect, making forecasts more synchronized with the observed system. On the other hand, when the parametrization is removed, the tendency for the event to be forecasted prematurely increases.

It is possible to observe the influence of the cold pool parameterization on the event's intensity. Looking at the pressure, the parameterization performs well during the first 24 hours of forecast (CP-ON and CP-15km), unlike the ERA5 and CP-OFF forecasts. Later, between 24 and 60 hours, the low-pressure system intensifies, and the CP-1HD050 experiment is the one that best reproduces this behavior. The other parameterization settings make the event significantly more intense than observed, and both CP-OFF and ERA5 underestimate its intensity. Additionally, when the hurricane approaches landfall along the Texas coast, the forecasts are very close to the observed data. Once the hurricane moves inland (forecast times beyond 54 hours), a slight underestimation is seen in the simulations that include the cold pool process, with the most accurate being the default configuration (CP-ON) and the higher resolution setup (CP-15km). It is worth noting that although the track diverges from the observation over this continental region (Figure \ref{fig:trajectbest}), CP-ON stands out by producing intensity values closer to the observed.

Regarding the observed wind, similar comments apply: during the first 24 hours of forecast, simulations with cold pool parameterization overestimate wind intensity, placing the system up to one category above the observed, except for the simulation using the optimal configuration (CP-1HD050). Between 24 and 60 hours of forecast, the CP-1HD050 curve aligns most closely with the observed values, while both ERA5 and CP-OFF tend to underestimate the storm's intensification by up to one category, and the other cold pool parameterization configurations overestimate it by up to two categories. However, unlike what occurs with pressure, after landfall (between 66 and 72 hours of forecast), wind speeds predicted by the cold pool parameterization and ERA5 overestimate the observed values, while the CP-OFF curve is the one that most closely matches reality.

The trends observed in Figure \ref{fig:compfore} are confirmed by these panels, for both the minimum MSLP and the maximum wind speed near the central pressure. It is noteworthy that, once again, ERA5 values are considerably higher than observed, with an average pressure bias of approximately +7 hPa and wind speed errors of around 25 km/h. Resolution also proves to be an important factor, for both pressure and wind, reducing wind errors by up to 33\% in the near-surface wind field.

Interestingly, even though it was initialized later, CP-25 shows poorer performance compared to the other forecasts initialized on September 24. We propose two hypotheses for this outcome. The first is that the initial 12-hour spin-up period was not discarded for this simulation, and, as shown in Figure 6.10, CP-25 significantly diverges from the observed data. The second is that the bias stems from the initial condition itself, as suggested by Figure \ref{fig:trackingmetoffice}. According to the forecast produced by the Met Office, when initialized on that date, the model does not perform as well as the others, likely due to its proximity to the hurricane's rapid intensification that occurred at 12 UTC on the same day. To confirm these hypotheses, ensemble testing would be necessary, which we leave for future work.

Finally, when compared to Hurricane Beryl, we observe some differences, particularly in the magnitude of the errors and the behavior of ERA5, especially regarding wind intensity. While ERA5 produced a satisfactory wind intensity forecast for Hurricane Helene, it showed a strong bias for Beryl. Furthermore, although CP-OFF is not as far from CP-ON in this case, the importance of cold pools remains evident, particularly when analyzing the forecast from the CP-1HD050 configuration.

\subsection{Rainfall}

In this subsection, we present the results of the experiments' performance concerning the rainfall. As was done for Hurricane Beryl, this analysis is divided into three components: pattern and spatial rainfall distribution, rainfall mean and overall distribution, and rainfall extremes.

However, in contrast to Hurricane Beryl, the storm’s lifetime over the oceanic region was shorter. Additionally, the interaction with other synoptic-scale systems over land and the influence of orographic features led to extreme precipitation over the continental region, particularly across the Carolinas, as discussed in Section \ref{section61}. Therefore, we divided the analysis between the portion over the United States, where NCEP data are used as the reference, and the portion outside the United States, where GSMaP data serve as the reference.

This choice was based on the observation that GPM-IMERG data, especially daily accumulations, significantly underestimated the observed values (see Table  \ref{tab:highest_rainfall_state}). This preliminary assessment is available in the appendix. Finally, to support this separation of continental analysis, the author developed a land mask, which is available on GitHub\footnote{\url{https://github.com/biancafusi/Dissertation}}.

The metrics presented in this section are calculated based on the accumulated rainfall fields over this period. Accordingly, Figure \ref{fig:ets} displays the Equitable Threat Score (ETS) for accumulated precipitation over (a) the United States and (b) outside the United States.

The first noticeable difference between the two panels is the presence of higher rainfall thresholds in (a) compared to (b), which reflects the occurrence of extreme rainfall over the continental region. The second distinction lies in the overall ETS values, which tend to be higher in (a) than in (b). We hypothesize that this is due to the larger spatial domain in (b), which results in more possible points for correct or incorrect predictions, thereby increasing the likelihood of lower scores.

Over the United States region, when considering thresholds above 0.22 mm, a clear trend emerges. ERA5 and GPM-IMERG data perform well in reproducing light to moderate rainfall but show a sharp decline in performance at extreme thresholds when compared to NCEP data. Regarding the effect of cold pool parameterization, CP-ON shows slightly higher scores than CP-OFF for thresholds up to 2.26 mm/h. However, for moderate to heavy rainfall, the parameterization appears to reduce forecast skill. This deficiency is partially addressed by the optimal configuration (CP-1HD050), which improves performance but still exhibits similar biases to CP-OFF for some thresholds, except for the highest extremes, which will be discussed later. Interestingly, CP-25 also slightly improves forecast skill compared to the other configurations, despite larger errors in track and intensity forecasts. Increasing resolution (CP-15km) does not appear to substantially impact the ETS, as its performance remains close to CP-ON for moderate and extreme thresholds.

In panel (b), a similar trend is observed for lower thresholds up to 0.48 mm/h. Beyond this point, the inclusion of cold pool processes tends to reduce the ETS scores. For moderate to extreme thresholds (above 23 mm/h), this divergence decreases, but a reduction in forecast performance is still observed. Despite this, with proper tuning of the parameterization components, improvements can be achieved, particularly in the CP-1HD050 and CP-25 experiments. As expected, ERA5 and GPM-IMERG show better overall performance in this oceanic region. However, for thresholds below 2 mm/h, ERA5 exhibits a decline in performance. 

We hypothesize that this is due to the scarcity of meteorological observations over oceanic areas, which leads to increased uncertainty and potential biases in the reanalysis data.

These trends are further confirmed by the computation of the Pearson correlation coefficient, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:spatially}.

Furthermore, an important feature observed both here and in Figure \ref{fig:ets} is that the CP-OFF configuration performs better outside the United States, showing slightly higher correlation values than the experiments that include cold pool parameterization, unlike the performance observed over the United States region. Among the cold pool configurations, CP-25 presents the best spatial rainfall pattern performance, followed in descending order by CP-1HD050, CP-15km, and CP-ON. Despite this ranking, the differences in correlation values among model configurations are relatively small, with variations of only 0.06 and 0.03 between the highest and lowest correlations over and outside the United States, respectively.

\section{Discussion of key outcomes}

Overall, the performance of the MONAN experiments in reproducing the storm trajectory was comparable to that of HAFS and other models, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:forecast2}. The main differences among forecasts emerged over continental regions, particularly in the CP-ON experiment. However, these discrepancies were significantly reduced by parameter tuning, as demonstrated by the optimal configuration (CP-1HD050), and by increasing the horizontal resolution to 15 km.

Trajectory errors, calculated as the great-circle distance between the forecasted and observed central pressure positions, were generally small, particularly when compared to those found for Hurricane Beryl. For example, the worst-performing forecast during Helene reached an error of approximately 80 km, whereas for Beryl, errors approached 300 km. This contrast is likely due to the longer forecast window for Beryl (6 days) versus Helene (3 days). Time series and mean error analyses further indicate when and how these forecast deviations evolve. After 36 hours of forecast lead time, CP-OFF shows a rapid increase in positional error exceeding 60 km, while forecasts accounting for cold pool effects (CP-ON) reach that threshold later, at 42-54 hours. Notably, CP-25 and CP-15km did not reach such error magnitudes. As expected, ERA5 maintained low errors throughout the period; however, CP-15km even outperformed ERA5 between 42 and 54 hours of lead time. Most discrepancies became more evident after landfall, beyond the 54-hour forecast window.

Experiments CP-15km, CP-1HD050, and CP-25 provide valuable insight into trajectory prediction improvement through increased resolution, optimal cold pool parameterization, and appropriate initialization timing, the latter extensively discussed in \citeonline{yamaguchi2012tropical}. We observed a reduction in trajectory error of up to 49.5\% when cold pool processes and increased horizontal resolution were incorporated, a feature not observed during Beryl's forecast period. 

Throughout Helene’s trajectory, forecasts tended to shift eastward relative to the reference during the oceanic phase (until 36 hours). Near landfall, however, the trajectories began exhibiting a westward bias, which persisted in the CP-25, CP-15km, and CP-ON experiments. In contrast, forecasts from CP-1HD050 and CP-OFF reverted to an eastward shift, more closely aligning with the pattern observed in ERA5 data.

Regarding phase accuracy, cold pool parameterization appeared to enhance phase alignment with observations. In contrast, forecasts without this process (CP-OFF) demonstrated a growing phase mismatch, particularly after 36 hours of lead time, resulting in a premature forecast relative to the observed trajectory. This feature is observed in both Helene and Beryl, so we hypothesize that this could be related to de model systematic errors.

Contrary to what was observed in the forecasts during the Beryl period, the model predictions were able to more accurately capture the trends in both mean sea level pressure (MSLP) and near-surface wind during Helene. In this context, the optimal configuration stands out, exhibiting the lowest errors in both pressure and wind fields, underscoring the sensitivity of cold pool parameterization to the pressure field within the model. We hypothesize that the underestimation of storm intensity in Beryl was influenced by the relatively weak intensity captured by ERA5, which provided the initial conditions for both hurricane simulations. This, however, does not appear to be the case here.

It was observed that ERA5 underestimates the pressure, particularly during the most intense phase of the hurricane near the Texas coastline. Similarly, when the cold pool effect is not considered (CP-OFF), an underestimation is also present. This allows us to propose a new hypothesis: that the cold pool effect may influence the pressure field through interactions with the model dynamics, even if such interference is not directly evident. In other words, although the buoyancy-excess variable does not evolve in direct correlation with pressure changes, its role cannot be dismissed. Due to the complexity of this interaction, a detailed analysis lies beyond the scope of this dissertation and is suggested for future research.

Regarding the quantitative errors, the optimal configuration showed relatively low values, with an average error of approximately 3.5 hPa for MSLP and 20 km/h for the near-surface wind. It is also noteworthy that ERA5 does not exhibit a significant wind bias in this case, with errors around 25 km/h. One hypothesis for this behavior concerns the systematic tracking error: for Helene, the spatial domain analyzed was more constrained and focused, while for Beryl, a broader area was assessed. Moreover, Beryl remained over the ocean for most of its lifecycle, where wind measurements are primarily estimated rather than directly observed. This limitation in observational data over the ocean may introduce a systematic bias in the reanalysis data assimilation process.

In this analysis of the produced rainfall, we broaden the temporal scope to include the entirety of September 28th. Additionally, we incorporate two distinct reference datasets for comparative purposes: the National Centers for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) dataset for data over the United States, and the Global Satellite Mapping of Precipitation (GSMaP) dataset for regions outside the United States.

Overall, regarding the performance in reproducing the hurricane’s rainfall pattern, forecasts produced using MONAN generally exhibit a moderate correlation across the entire domain. Over the continental United States, the metrics indicate a stronger correlation, whereas in the surrounding oceanic regions, the correlation tends to be more moderate. This discrepancy is likely due to the difference in area size between the two domains: the oceanic region encompasses a broader area, offering more grid points for spatial correlation, which tends to reduce the overall values. According to the Equitable Threat Score (ETS), the CP-25, CP-15km, and CP-OFF experiments performed best, particularly for light to moderate rainfall thresholds. Quantitatively, the inclusion of cold pool effects did not significantly alter the performance compared to CP-OFF, with variations of only 0.06 and 0.03 between the highest and lowest correlations over the United States and outside the United States, respectively.

Snapshots of key moments during Hurricane Helene’s evolution were produced to examine the spatial distribution of rainfall, also considering the morphological characteristics of the hurricane, such as the spiral rainbands and the eye structure. Consistent with findings from the Beryl case, precipitation appeared more spatially organized when cold pool processes were included. Additionally, the rainfall location on the day of the extreme precipitation event in North Carolina more closely matched observations when using the forecast from the optimal configuration.
When assessing the impact of horizontal resolution on rainfall distribution, we again observed the formation of spiral rainbands, which were most pronounced in the CP-15km experiment. This configuration also improved the organization of rainfall over the continent. These structures were also partially represented in the CP-ON experiment, albeit with less detail. A more in-depth investigation of the extreme rainfall event’s timing and location revealed a spatial bias in CP-ON, with precipitation displaced eastward. This was significantly improved in the CP-1HD050 experiment, which not only corrected the location of the extreme event but also captured structures that were absent in simulations, excluding cold pool effects.

The spatial distribution of biases can be further analyzed through Pearson correlation maps. These reveal that both CP-ON and CP-OFF exhibit bias over the northwestern region of Georgia, which was corrected in the CP-1HD050 experiment.

Examining cloud top temperatures during key stages of Helene’s lifecycle, we observed a similar pattern to that of Beryl. When cold pool effects are included, colder cloud tops, indicative of deeper convection, are present. Furthermore, the combination of cold pool representation and increased resolution enhances the depiction of features such as rainbands and the delineation of the hurricane’s eye region.

When evaluating the rainfall distribution, some differences emerge when compared to the results observed for Hurricane Beryl. Over the continental United States, the forecasts tend to follow the same distribution pattern as the NCEP data. However, outside the United States, there is an overestimation at moderate rainfall thresholds and an underestimation at more extreme thresholds. In terms of mean rainfall distribution, there is overall agreement between the forecasts and NCEP data over land. Yet, in oceanic regions, the forecasts tend to overestimate the mean precipitation when compared to GSMaP. Over the United States, the average rainfall remains around 0.8 mm/h, with strong consistency among all experiments. In contrast, over oceanic areas, the GSMaP mean is approximately 0.3 mm/h. The closest forecast is CP-OFF, with a mean of 0.5 mm/h, followed by the cold pool parameterized experiments, which tend to overestimate, averaging around 0.8 mm/h.

A more detailed insight into the hurricane-related precipitation is obtained by analyzing rainfall extremes, represented here by the 85th percentile of the sample distribution. Over the continental United States, the 95th percentile threshold reaches 7.3 mm/h and is accurately captured by all experiments, with discrepancies ranging from only 0.4\% to 1\%. Over the oceanic region, CP-OFF stands out as the best-performing experiment, consistent with other metrics. However, an improvement in rainfall distribution is observed in the cold pool parameterization experiments, particularly in the CP-1HD050 configuration, which reflects enhanced tuning of cold pool parameters.

Interestingly, when these metrics are computed specifically within the vicinity of the hurricane (i.e., within a more confined, storm-centered area), the differences between CP-OFF and the parameterized experiments become less pronounced. In some cases, forecasts using cold pool parameterizations even outperform CP-OFF (Figure \ref{fig:samesub}A). This suggests that large-scale precipitation patterns might be influencing the overall evaluation, potentially introducing a systematic bias in this metric. Such bias could be mitigated by adopting a more targeted evaluation framework, as proposed in \citeonline{marchok2007validation} and further applied in \citeonline{brackins2020evaluation}).

Overall, the analyses conducted confirm that MONAN is capable of capturing the observed extreme rainfall associated with the hurricane. The cold pool parameterization contributes significantly to improving the morphological aspects of the storm, such as its structure and location. Regarding the rainfall magnitude, experiments including cold pool effects tend to produce deeper convection and colder cloud tops. In terms of spatial distribution, a strong correlation is found over the United States, often surpassing ERA5 and GPM-IMERG, especially in extreme precipitation events. Nevertheless, in oceanic regions, a tendency toward underestimation of rainfall intensity remains a limitation, potentially caused by a systematic bias, and can be addressed in future work.
